## The message spine

**The one sentence the piece defends.** Among wines that drinkers rate the same, Burgundy's name adds most of the price.

**What a reader should be able to repeat afterwards.** Burgundy's villages bought their own prestige in the 1800s by borrowing their best vineyards' names, and today that name costs several times what the same rated pleasure costs from Beaujolais or Oregon.

**What the piece must never sound like.** An attack on expensive wine. Every section should leave room for Burgundy at its best being worth the money, which is also what the evidence shows.

**The through-line device.** A name. Section 1 is a name being acquired, section 2 is what the name costs, sections 3 to 5 test whether the name is carrying anything real, section 6 is a name being acquired again. Keep the word "name" working across the piece and the structure holds itself together.

## Section 1. The villages that renamed themselves

**Objective.** Open in a story so the reader arrives at the data already interested. Establish that the name premium was built deliberately and long ago.

**Insight to land.** Burgundy's villages attached the names of their most famous vineyards to their own, so that ordinary wine from the commune could trade on a grand cru's reputation.

**Facts to use.**

| Fact | Detail |
| :-- | :-- |
| Gevrey petitioned the king | 1847, to add Chambertin |
| Chambolle followed | 1882, taking Musigny |
| Morey | took Saint-Denis |
| Scale | around a dozen Côte d'Or communes. **Say "around a dozen"**, since sources give 11 and 12 |
| Verifiable examples | Gevrey-Chambertin, Chambolle-Musigny, Morey-Saint-Denis, Vosne-Romanée, Puligny-Montrachet, Chassagne-Montrachet |

**Message pointers.**

- Lead with the act rather than the explanation. A village asking a king for permission to use a vineyard's name is the image that does the work.
- Let the reader notice that the names they already half-know are hyphenated. The hook lands on recognition, so give them Gevrey-Chambertin and Chambolle-Musigny before explaining why.
- Resist explaining the premium in this section. The job here is to make the reader wonder what a name is worth.
- End on the question the data answers, so section 2 arrives as a payoff.

**Visual block: PHOTO A.**

- A Gevrey-Chambertin scene: the village sign, the Chambertin vineyard, or any road sign carrying a hyphenated village name. A sign showing the hyphen makes the section visual on its own
- Credit line under the image, as the licence requires

**Length.** 130 words.

**Do not.** State a hard village count. Imply the practice was deceptive; it was a legal, public commercial decision.

## Section 2. What the name costs today

**Objective.** The core measurement, delivered once and cleanly.

**Insight to land.** Among wines that drinkers rate the same, the Burgundy name multiplies the price several times over.

**Facts to use.** All Vivino, US market, restricted to 2020 and later vintages. State the restriction once.

| Group | Median price at 4.0 to 4.2 | Share under $50 among 4.0+ |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Burgundy village and above | $120 | 0% to 2.5% depending on pull |
| Bourgogne regional | $60 to $68 | 30% to 39% |
| Central Otago Pinot | $54 | 33% |
| Beaujolais cru | $45 | 71% |
| Willamette Pinot | $35 | 65% |
| Beaujolais regional | $34 | 80% |

**Headline sentence should carry the share rather than the median.** A share is harder to argue with.

**Decide before drafting.** Which pull the piece cites. The 9 October cross-section gives 2.5% for Burgundy village and above; the 12 October pull gives 0%. Pick one, state its sample size, and use it consistently.

**Ratio to quote.** At 4.0 to 4.2, Burgundy village and above costs 3.4 times a Willamette Pinot, 2.7 times a Beaujolais cru and 2.2 times a Central Otago Pinot.

**Visual block: INTERACTIVE 1.**

- Working name: **Where the name premium shows up**
- Proposed slug: `name-premium`, to live at `corktotable.co/visuals/name-premium`
- Form: horizontal bars, share under $50 by group, Burgundy last. Second view toggles to median price at matched rating
- Title in sentence case. Single footer: "Wine rating and price data obtained from Vivino, US market, October 2026."
- Add the slug to the `VISUALS` map in `next.config.js` when the file is built, and confirm the branded URL returns 200 before it goes in the post. The proxy was verified working on 15 October against the Piece 2 `price-quality` visual

**Visual block: DATA BOX A (static).**

- Stat: **0%** or **2.5%**, whichever pull is chosen
- Body: share of Burgundy village wines rated 4.0 or better that cost under $50, against 65% for Willamette Valley Pinot Noir
- Source line: "Wine rating and price data obtained from Vivino, US market, October 2026."

**Message pointers.**

- Give one number early and let it sit. The share is the whole section, so do not bury it after the method.
- Anchor it in a decision the reader recognises: two bottles, same rating from thousands of drinkers, one shelf.
- Name the vintage restriction once, plainly, and move on. A method sentence that apologises invites doubt.
- Do not walk through all six groups in prose. Two contrasts in the text, the rest in the chart.
- The ratios (3.4, 2.7, 2.2 times) are more quotable than the medians. Use one of them.

**Length.** 200 words.

**Honest note to include.** Otago sits closer to Burgundy than the others. A third of its highly rated wines come in under $50, against two thirds for Willamette, so it belongs as a quality comparison rather than the budget answer.

## Section 3. Is it the wine or the label

**Objective.** Show that both obvious objections were tested, and that they pull in opposite directions.

**Insight to land.** One bias flatters expensive wine and one would have punished Burgundy. Testing found the first live and the second absent.

**Facts to use.**

| Test | Result | Reading |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Price halo, carried from Piece 2 | Revealed price lifted ratings by **+0.40**, and the effect vanished blind | Expensive Burgundy's crowd ratings are flattered, so the premium is understated |
| Maturation test, new | 40 Burgundies, every vintage 2015 to 2024, 282 rated rows. Older vintages rate **-0.065** against newer, older higher in only **8 of 31** wines | Burgundy's ratings are not depressed by being drunk young |
| Split by tier | Village and above flat at -0.027. Entry Bourgogne at **-0.160**, older higher in 1 of 9 | Cheap Bourgogne kept too long is the only group that suffers |

**Visual block: STATIC CHART 2.**

- Working name: **Older vintages do not rate higher**
- Form: dot plot, the 31 within-wine differences around a zero line, cluster sitting slightly negative
- Static only, no interactive build needed. Safe to build now, since it uses ratings alone and no price data
- Footer: "Wine rating data obtained from Vivino, October 2026."

**Visual block: QUOTE BOX A.**

- **P3-Q7**, score 21. Use paragraph 2, on the marginal increase in quality shrinking while the price function does the opposite
- Pairs with Castriota et al. (2022), who find the price premium statistically significant only above 90 points across 266,301 Wine Spectator reviews. A drinker and an econometrician reaching the same shape is the strongest move available here

**Message pointers.**

- Frame the section as having tried to break the finding twice. That earns the reader's trust more than any caveat.
- Take the ageing objection first. It is the one a Burgundy lover reaches for, and the one that could have sunk the finding, so meeting it head on signals good faith.
- Then the price halo, with a link back to Piece 2. It works in the piece's favour: the label lifts Burgundy's scores, so the measured premium is if anything an understatement.
- The maturation test is the original contribution. One sentence on what was done, one on what came back. Resist the method detail.
- The line that lands: the two biases point in opposite directions, and the one that would have sunk the finding is simply not there.
- A drinker and an econometrician arriving at the same curve independently is the strongest moment in the piece. Let the quote and the study sit next to each other without explaining the coincidence.

**Length.** 170 words.

## Section 4. What the critics say

**Objective.** Show the pattern repeating in a source that has nothing to do with Vivino.

**Insight to land.** Professional critics score Beaujolais and New World Pinot highly at a fraction of Burgundy's price, and their own value lists cannot include Burgundy.

**Facts to use.** Wine Enthusiast, retrieved 9 October 2026.

| Set | Scores | Median price | Note |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Beaujolais, 26 wines | 90 to 96 points | **$24** | 23 of 26 at or under $30. The one wine near $100 scored the highest, 96 |
| Best value Pinot Noir, 10 wines | 90 to 93 points | **$17** | **Zero Burgundy entries.** Regions: Willamette (3), California (2), Pays d'Oc (2), Victoria, San Luis Obispo Coast, Monterey County |

**Visual block: DATA BOX B (static).**

- Stat: **$24**
- Body: median price of 26 Beaujolais wines scored 90 to 96 points, with 23 of them at or under $30
- Source line: "Wine score and price data obtained from Wine Enthusiast, October 2026."

**Community voice, inline rather than boxed.**

- **P3-Q1**, score 96. First two paragraphs only, on Burgundy having more in common with baseball cards than with beer, and value resting on rarity and collectability. Mark the cut with […]. Paragraphs 3 and 4 are excluded by house rule

**Message pointers.**

- The absence does the work. A professional value list for Pinot Noir with no Burgundy on it says more than any median.
- Keep the two scales separate and say why in half a sentence. Readers trust a writer who declines an easy comparison.
- Resist naming wines. One median, one absence, and move on.
- The baseball-cards quote belongs here rather than earlier, because by this point the reader has the numbers to agree with it.

**Length.** 120 words.

**Guardrail.** Never convert between the scales. Critic 90+ and Vivino 4.0+ are different thresholds. Report each on its own terms and let the reader see the shape repeat.

## Section 5. The case against

**Objective.** Carry the real objections in the open, which is what makes the rest credible.

**Insight to land.** Several of the obvious defences of the premium are genuine, and none of them accounts for its size.

**Facts to use.**

| Objection | Evidence | How to handle |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Our own sample is skewed | The crowd's Burgundy is largely supermarket bottles under $80 | **Quote P3-Q2, score 48.** Let the community state our limitation |
| The merchant matters more than the region | A random Burgundy from a serious shop at $60 to $100 would likely be very good | **Quote P3-Q17, score 18.** The best rebuttal from the other side |
| Land genuinely costs that much | Côte-d'Or premier cru Pinot Noir averaged **€1.15m per hectare** in 2025, up 11%, and premier cru Chardonnay €2.7m | One sentence with the figure, and note that land price follows bottle price, so it is partly circular |
| Terroir is real and measurable | Two vineyards under 2 km apart, one producer, gave distinguishable chemical fingerprints | Concede it in one line. Flag as amber: 34 samples, one producer, vintage effect larger than site |
| Burgundy's best wins blind | At the Judgment of Princeton, 2012, Drouhin's Beaune Clos des Mouches was the **only** white statistically distinguishable from the field | Give it fully. It is the strongest pro-Burgundy fact available |
| And in the same tasting | A Bâtard-Montrachet Grand Cru tied for sixth with a New Jersey Chardonnay; a Meursault-Charmes Premier Cru finished tenth of ten; ranks two to ten were statistically indistinguishable | Give both halves. The nuance is what makes it land |

**Visual block: QUOTE BOX B.**

- **P3-Q8**, score 28. Use in full: mostly overpriced, occasionally transcendent. This carries the house price-neutrality rule in a drinker's voice and protects the piece from reading as an attack

**Message pointers.**

- Open by conceding. The strongest version of this piece is the one that has already argued against itself.
- Lead with the Reddit objection in its own words, because it indicts our own data and a reader can see we did not hide it.
- Land cost and terroir get one line each. On land, note that the price of a vineyard follows the price of the bottle, so the defence partly circles back on itself.
- Give Princeton both halves in consecutive sentences and let the reader feel the nuance rather than being told it is nuanced.
- Close on the overpriced-and-occasionally-transcendent quote. It does the price-neutrality work better than anything we could write.

**Length.** 180 words. This is the section most likely to overrun; the table above is deliberately longer than the prose should be.

**Do not.** Imply prices were published alongside the Judgment of Princeton results. They were not, so no price-per-point comparison.

## Section 6. A premium being made right now

**Objective.** Close forward, on a decision that has not happened yet, so the mechanism stops being historical.

**Insight to land.** The same process that renamed Gevrey in 1847 is running today, and it can be watched on a specific bottle.

**Facts to use.**

| Fact | Detail |
| :-- | :-- |
| Marsannay's standing | Regional Bourgogne appellation only until **1987**, when the village appellation was created |
| The push | Growers began working towards premier cru in **2002** |
| Where it stands | The producers' body asked the INAO to elevate **14 climats**. Still pending at the most recent reading, January 2026 |
| The bottle | **Les Grasses Têtes** is one of the 14. Our own dataset holds Domaine Bruno Clair's Marsannay Les Grasses Têtes across nine vintages, rated **4.0 to 4.3**, listed at **$117** |
| The move | If the promotion lands, the words on the label change and the liquid stays as it is |

**Community voice, inline.**

- **P3-Q18**, score 63. The 2019 to 2021 price rises shutting a generation out of mid-upper Burgundy. The emotional centre of the whole topic
- Alternative if a lighter close is wanted: **P3-Q19**, score 124, closing line only, "Everyone deserves good wine."

**Message pointers.**

- Switch to the present tense. Something is about to happen, and that is why this closes the piece.
- Name the bottle and its price, so the mechanism stops being an abstraction and becomes an object on a shelf.
- The closing idea: the label changes and the liquid in the bottle stays exactly as it is. State it plainly and stop; no flourish.
- One quote, then end. A short piece that keeps going after its last idea reads as padding.

**Visual block: PHOTO B.**

- Marsannay or northern Côte de Nuits vineyards. A shot that reads as ordinary farmland suits a section about a name changing while the ground stays put
- Credit line under the image, as the licence requires

**Length.** 130 words.

**Check before publishing.** Whether the INAO decree has landed, and that the Bruno Clair bottling is from the candidate climat rather than a like-named plot. Both are open items.

## Section 7. If you are buying this week

**Objective.** Give the reader a use for all of it. This is what makes a short piece shareable.

**Insight to land.** Producer beats appellation, and the savings sit in the less famous names.

**Facts and voices to use.**

- **P3-Q14**, score 11, inline: better off spending the money on a lesser village from a better producer
- **P3-Q15**, score 6: producer, vineyard, vintage, in that order. Low score, so present as practical advice rather than consensus
- Named value routes from **P3-Q13**, score 10, as research rather than quotation: Savigny-lès-Beaune as the best value village in the region, the Côte Chalonnaise, Hautes-Côtes de Nuits, Marsannay, and the reliable négociant houses
- Beaujolais cru and Willamette Valley as the direct substitutes, with the critic figures from section 4 as the warrant

**Message pointers.**

- Three concrete moves, no hedging. A reader who has come this far wants instructions.
- Producer over village is the one transferable rule. Say it once, cleanly.
- Name regions and villages rather than individual wines, so the advice survives the vintage.
- No sign-off summarising the piece. The buying advice is the ending.

**Length.** 90 words.
