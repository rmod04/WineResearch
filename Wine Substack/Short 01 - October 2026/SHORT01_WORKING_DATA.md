## 1. What the piece argues

Burgundy carries a price premium that the name earns rather than the glass. Among wines that drinkers rate equally well, a Burgundy village bottling costs several times what a Beaujolais cru, a Willamette Pinot or a Central Otago Pinot costs.

The piece stays neutral on whether that premium is deserved. Scarcity, land cost, hand harvesting and genuine ceiling quality are all real. What the data shows is the size of the gap at matched ratings, and where a drinker can find the same rated pleasure for less.

**Read section 4 before writing any number in this document into a draft.** The finding holds. A first price check on 12 October looked as though it undermined the magnitude; a second pass the same day showed that check was flawed and the magnitude stands, with one correction. Section 4.1 has the full account.

## 3. The maturation test

### 3.1 Why it was run

A reasonable objection to section 2: crowd raters mostly drink wine young, and Burgundy is built to age. If so, Burgundy's ratings are depressed by immaturity and the measured premium is overstated. Kopsacheilis et al. (2024) give grounds for the worry, finding that amateur raters respond to immediate enjoyment.

The test: hold the wine constant and vary the vintage. If young-drinking depresses Burgundy's ratings, older vintages, now mature, should rate higher than recent ones.

### 3.2 Method

40 red Burgundy wines, all vintages from 2015 to 2024, 335 wine-vintage rows, 282 of them carrying a rating. Within each wine, mean rating of the 2015 to 2017 vintages compared against mean rating of the 2020 to 2023 vintages. 31 wines had at least two rated vintages on each side.

### 3.3 Result

Older vintages do **not** rate higher.

- Mean difference, older minus newer: **-0.065** rating points
- Median difference: -0.033
- Older rates higher in **8 of 31** wines, lower in 22, within 0.05 in 15

Split by tier, the pattern is sharper:

| Tier | n wines | Mean difference (older minus newer) | Older rates higher |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bourgogne regional | 9 | **-0.160** | 1 of 9 |
| Village and above | 22 | -0.027 | 7 of 22 |

Village and above is flat. Entry-level Bourgogne is where older vintages rate materially worse, which fits wines kept past their window rather than wines misjudged young.

### 3.4 The 2016 cross-section

Restricting to the 2016 vintage alone, roughly ten years old and genuinely mature:

| Tier | n | Median rating | Median price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bourgogne regional | 9 | 3.80 | $37 |
| Village and above | 25 | 4.20 | $147 |
| Premier cru | 2 | 4.25 | $302 |

The rating hierarchy survives at matched maturity. Village wines do rate better than regional, by 0.40.

### 3.5 What this earns the piece

The maturation objection can be answered with a measurement. This test uses ratings only, so the price problem in section 4.1 leaves it intact. It is currently the most robust original finding in the piece.

### 3.6 A constraint found while running it

**Vivino reports one price per wine across every vintage.** Checked across all 40 wines: zero showed more than one price. Claude Dugat Gevrey-Chambertin is $780 whether the vintage is 2015 or 2023.

Consequences:
- No vintage-level price analysis is possible from this source. Do not imply one.
- The 2016 cross-section above compares 2016 ratings against wine-level prices. State that if the price column is shown at all.
- Any claim about older vintages costing more needs a different source, such as auction or merchant data.

## 4. Evidence against the hypothesis

Run deliberately, as a study would. Each item is graded by how much damage it does, and whether it can be handled in a sentence or needs real work.

### 4.1 Vivino's prices. DOWNGRADED from SERIOUS to MANAGEABLE on 12 October (second pass).

**The first pass overstated this, and the correction matters more than the original claim.**

On 12 October a first check compared Vivino's Faiveley Nuits-Saint-Georges at $199.99 against wine.com's $89.99 and read it as a 2.2 times pricing error. A later pull of the same wines with vintage years attached showed why that was wrong: **Vivino's row for that wine is the 2001 vintage.** A 2001 village Nuits-Saint-Georges trades well above the current release, so the two figures were never comparable. The comparison was at fault rather than the price.

Of the three original checks, only one now stands as a like-for-like comparison, and it matched:

| Wine or set | Vivino | Independent | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Louis Latour Gevrey-Chambertin | $98.95 | $97.99 lowest of 8 retailers | **Matches** |
| Joseph Drouhin Bourgogne Pinot Noir | $32.14 | $27.95 lowest of 6 retailers | Close |
| Faiveley Nuits-Saint-Georges | $199.99 (2001 vintage) | $89.99 (2019 vintage) | **Not comparable.** Withdrawn |
| Beaujolais at 90+ points | cru median $45 | $24 median release price | **Not comparable.** Release price against market price |

### 4.1b The real defect, measured

The genuine issue is narrower and now quantified. Vivino carries one price per wine, and the vintage shown on a given row is whatever vintage that record happens to hold. A minority of rows are old vintages priced at aged-market levels.

A fresh pull on 12 October, 110 red Burgundy rows and 150 Beaujolais rows with vintage years attached, measures the size of it.

**Pre-2020 rows are the expensive ones.** Median price of the 15 pre-2020 Burgundy rows is **$299.95**, against **$81.95** for the 95 rows from 2020 on. Those old rows include a 2001 Vosne-Romanée at $6,152 and a 2002 Chambolle-Musigny at $1,412.

**They are too few to drive the comparison.** Pre-2020 rows are 13.6% of the Burgundy sample and 6.7% of the Beaujolais sample.

**Restricting every group to 2020 and later changes the answer by about a tenth:**

| Group | Median at 4.0 to 4.2, all vintages | Restricted to 2020+ | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burgundy village and above | $133.99 (n=55) | **$119.98 (n=46)** | -10.5% |
| Bourgogne regional | $67.79 (n=10) | $67.79 (n=10) | 0.0% |
| Beaujolais | $44.79 (n=57) | $43.89 (n=54) | -2.0% |

The restricted Burgundy figure, $119.98, lands within a dollar of the $120 reported in section 2 from the earlier pull. **The headline number survives.**

**And the lead statistic survives more comfortably than before.** In this fresh sample, among wines rated 4.0 or better and restricted to 2020+ vintages:

| Group | Share under $50 |
|---|---|
| Beaujolais | **68.5%** (37 of 54) |
| Bourgogne regional | 30.0% (3 of 10) |
| Burgundy village and above | **0.0%** (0 of 49) |

The earlier sample gave 2.5% for Burgundy village and above. This one gives zero. Report the figure from whichever dataset the piece actually uses, and state the vintage restriction.

### 4.1c What to do about it

1. **Restrict every group to 2020 and later vintages** before computing any published figure. That removes the artefact at no cost and is the whole fix.
2. **Make no vintage-level price claim**, since the price is wine-level.
3. **Spot-check two or three named wines per group** against an accessible retailer at draft stage, matching on vintage. This is a sanity check rather than a rebuild.
4. **Drop the plan to build a full Wine Enthusiast price layer.** It was the right response to a problem that turned out to be mostly mine. Wine Enthusiast's searchable database does not expose prices in bulk, its curated lists are price-capped by construction, and the major retailers that would give clean market prices block automated access. The vintage restriction does the necessary work instead.

### 4.2 Land costs are real. MODERATE, explainable.

Safer transaction data reported by Decanter, published 22 May 2026, for 2025: Côte-d'Or premier cru Chardonnay averaged **€2.7 million per hectare**, premier cru Pinot Noir **€1.15 million**, up 11% on the year.

A grower paying that cannot sell cheaply. The piece must not pretend the cost side is invented.

The honest qualification is that land price is substantially a consequence of bottle price rather than only a cause. Buyers bid for vineyards on the strength of what the wine fetches, so citing land cost to justify bottle cost is partly circular. One sentence in the text, with the figure, and the circularity noted.

### 4.3 Terroir differences are chemically detectable. LOW to MODERATE.

Roullier-Gall, C., Lucio, M., Noret, L., Schmitt-Kopplin, P., & Gougeon, R. D. (2014). How Subtle Is the "Terroir" Effect? Chemistry-Related Signatures of Two "Climats de Bourgogne". *PLoS ONE*, 9(5), e97615.

Pinot Noir from two vineyards under 2 km apart, in Flagey-Echezeaux and Vosne-Romanée, worked by a single producer, showed distinguishable high-resolution mass-spectrometry fingerprints. Several methods agreed.

Limits that matter here:

- 34 samples in total, and wines analysed for one vintage only
- One producer, one vineyard pair, so nothing generalises to other climats
- Separation was clearest in the grapes and weaker in the finished wines
- Vintage had a larger effect than site
- Most discriminant compounds were never identified
- Samples were supplied by Domaine de la Romanée-Conti and the work was partly funded by regional wine bodies

**Assessment.** This supports the existence of site difference, which the piece should concede plainly. It says nothing about magnitude, and nothing in it addresses whether a detectable chemical difference is worth 3.4 times the money. Carry it in one or two sentences, because omitting it would look evasive to any reader who knows the literature.

### 4.4 Burgundy's best does win blind. LOW, and useful.

At the Judgment of Princeton in 2012, the white flight put Joseph Drouhin's Beaune Clos des Mouches first, and it was the only wine in that flight statistically distinguishable from the rest of the field.

**Assessment.** Good for the piece. The top of Burgundy earning its standing in a blind panel makes the rest of the argument harder to read as an attack on expensive wine.

### 4.5 Retailer choice may matter more than region. MODERATE.

P3-Q17 (score 18) argues that a random Burgundy from a serious merchant at $60 to $100 would likely be very good, and that bad impressions come from supermarket bottles. P3-Q2 (score 48) makes the same point about the crowd sample specifically.

**Assessment.** This cuts straight at the Vivino sample and cannot be measured away with the data in hand. Both quotes belong in the text, in the community's own words.

### 4.6 What survives

- **The direction of the premium.** Every independent source agrees that highly rated Beaujolais and New World Pinot cost a fraction of Burgundy. Critics score Beaujolais cru at 93 to 94 points for $23 to $29, and publish Pinot value lists with no Burgundy on them at all.
- **The maturation test**, which uses ratings only.
- **The historical and institutional mechanism** in section 8, which rests on records rather than on price data.
- **The magnitude, now tested.** Restricting to 2020+ vintages moves the Burgundy median by 10.5% and leaves the comparison intact. "Roughly three times" is supportable.

## 5. Critic layer

Vivino is read alongside critic scores, and the two are allowed to disagree. Most critic score and price pairs sit behind paywalls. Wine Enthusiast publishes full lists, so that is the usable source. Retrieved 9 October 2026.

### 5.1 Wine Enthusiast, Beaujolais, 26 wines with prices

- Scores 90 to 96 points
- Median price **$24**, range $11 to $97
- **23 of 26** priced at or under $30
- The top score, 96 points, is the one wine near $100

### 5.2 Wine Enthusiast, best value Pinot Noir, 10 wines

- Scores 90 to 93 points
- Median price **$17**, range $10 to $20
- Regions: Willamette Valley (3), California (2), Pays d'Oc (2), Victoria, San Luis Obispo Coast, Monterey County
- **Burgundy entries: zero**

The absence is structural, since the list is built on price, and it is still worth one sentence. When a major critic publishes a Pinot value list, Burgundy cannot appear on it.

### 5.3 Where critics and the crowd agree

Both put highly rated Beaujolais and New World Pinot at a fraction of Burgundy's price. Critics score Beaujolais cru at 93 to 94 points for $23 to $29. The crowd puts Beaujolais cru at 4.0 to 4.2 for a $45 median. Different scales, same shape.

### 5.4 Where to be careful

Critic 90+ point scores and Vivino 4.0+ ratings are not the same threshold and must never be converted into each other. Report each on its own scale and let the reader see the pattern repeat.

## 6. Academic layer

### 6.1 Quality pays off, but only at the very top

Castriota, S., Corsi, S., Frumento, P., & Ruggeri, G. (2022). Does quality pay off? "Superstar" wines and the uncertain price premium across quality grades. *Journal of Wine Economics*, 17(2), 141-158.

266,301 Wine Spectator reviews, 12 countries, US market, 1984 to 2019.

- The price premium is statistically significant only above 90 points. Between 50 and 90 points, price does not reliably differ by score.
- Price dispersion is U-shaped, widest at the bottom and the top of the quality scale.
- Chasing top quality is characterised as a risky strategy because the premium is volatile.

Use: the econometric version of what the community says in plain words. Pair with P3-Q7, whose author describes the same non-linear curve unprompted.

### 6.2 Appellation reputation is priced directly

Carew, R., & Florkowski, W. J. (2010). The Importance of Geographic Wine Appellations: Hedonic Pricing of Burgundy Wines in the British Columbia Wine Market. *Canadian Journal of Agricultural Economics*, 58(1), 93-108.

- Confirms collective reputation effects for the Burgundy region.
- Village designation carries a measurable independent price effect, larger for white than red.
- Named appellations including Chassagne-Montrachet Premier Cru, Meursault Premier Cru and Chablis Grand Cru carry the largest premiums.

Use: the direct academic warrant for the phrase "name premium". The appellation name itself is priced, separately from the wine. Caveat to state: the market studied is British Columbia, and the data is older.

### 6.3 Vivino's standing

Kopsacheilis, O., Analytis, P. P., Kaushik, K., Herzog, S. M., Bahrami, B., & Deroy, O. (2024). Crowdsourcing the assessment of wine quality: Vivino ratings, professional critics, and the weather. *Journal of Wine Economics*, 19(3), 285-304.

- Vivino correlates with critics at roughly 40%, against roughly 63% critic to critic.
- Vivino ratings respond to growing-season weather in the direction theory predicts, which is external validation.
- Scale: 280 million plus ratings, around 100,000 added daily, against roughly 20,000 wines a year for a major critic.
- Richard Hemming MW: influential with consumers, little traction in the trade.

Note that this paper validates Vivino's **ratings**. It is not evidence about Vivino's price field, which section 4.1 found unreliable. Do not let the citation cover both.

### 6.4 Carried from Piece 2

The price halo: revealed prices lifted ratings by 0.40 and the effect vanished blind. If anything this means expensive Burgundy's crowd ratings are flattered by its price tag, so the true premium at matched blind quality is probably **larger** than section 2 reports.

So among the rating biases, one is live and one was measured away. Both belong in the text.

## 8. Hooks for the casual reader

"Burgundy costs more" is not news to anybody who drinks wine. The data earns attention only once a reader has a reason to stay past the first chart. Three candidates, all verifiable, all independent of the price problem in section 4.1.

### Hook A: the villages that bought their own fame

In 1847 the village of Gevrey petitioned the king for permission to attach the name of its most famous vineyard, Chambertin, to its own. It became Gevrey-Chambertin. Chambolle followed in 1882, taking Musigny. Morey took Saint-Denis. Twelve communes of the Côte d'Or have fixed a celebrated vineyard name onto the village name, so that ordinary wine from the village could trade on it.

**Why it works.** The name premium was engineered on purpose, by the villages themselves, more than 175 years ago, and it worked so well that today's price gap is its direct descendant. The piece opens in a story and arrives at the data as the payoff.

**Strongest option for a short, accessible piece.** It needs no statistics to land, and it reframes the whole topic in two sentences.

**Before publishing.** Confirm the 1847 royal petition against a second source. Wikipedia is the current source and is not enough on its own. Jasper Morris's *Inside Burgundy* is the obvious place to check, and a community member recommends it by name in the quote pull.

### Hook B: the 2012 tasting nobody brings up

At the Judgment of Princeton, organised by wine economists, a panel tasted New Jersey wines blind against Burgundy and Bordeaux. In the whites, a Bâtard-Montrachet Grand Cru tied for sixth with a New Jersey Chardonnay. A Meursault-Charmes Premier Cru finished tenth of ten. Domaine Leflaive's Puligny-Montrachet came fifth, behind three New Jersey wines. The statisticians then concluded that the wines ranked second through tenth were indistinguishable from one another.

**Why it works.** Concrete, surprising, run by economists rather than journalists, and it carries its own nuance: the one wine that did stand out was also a Burgundy.

**Before publishing.** Cite Taber's report in the *Journal of Wine Economics*, 7(2), 2012. Prices were not published alongside the results, so do not imply any price-per-point comparison.

### Hook C: a name premium being created right now

Marsannay sits at the northern end of the Côte de Nuits. Its vines carried only the regional Bourgogne appellation until 1987, when the village appellation was created. Growers started working towards premier cru status in 2002. In 2024 the INAO named 14 climats as candidates, and as of late 2025 the final declaration was still pending.

One of those 14 is Les Grasses Têtes. Our own dataset holds Domaine Bruno Clair's Marsannay Les Grasses Têtes across nine vintages, rated 4.0 to 4.3, at a listed $117. If the promotion lands, the words on the label change and the liquid stays exactly as it is.

**Why it works.** It makes the mechanism testable and forward-looking, and it does so through a specific named wine already in our data. It also sets up a natural follow-up piece once the decision is published.

**Before publishing.** Check whether the INAO declaration has landed since October 2025, because that changes the tense of the entire hook. Also confirm the Bruno Clair bottling comes from the candidate climat rather than a similarly named plot elsewhere.

### Recommended combination

Open with **A**, land the data, close on **C**. **B** sits in the middle as the blind-tasting evidence. If the short format only has room for two, use **A** and **C**, since together they bracket the argument with a 19th-century decision and a pending one.

Related searches for "is burgundy wine worth the money" and "why is burgundy wine so expensive" return **"Burgundy color", "Burgundy red", "Burgundy hair"** and **"Wine Red"**. Google does not treat the word as unambiguously about wine.

### 9.7 Final recommendations, after the 15 October measurement

### 17.14 Section 5: three measures, defined once (27 October 2026)

Correction: earlier notes presented the 2.2x as an ageing measure. It is measure B below, which mixes ageing with the whole market rising.

| Measure | Question | What is compared | Burgundy | Comparison |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| A, the new release | How much dearer is a new release of the same wine now than a new release was then? | Hachette band, older vintage against recent vintage of the same wine, after inflation | 1.9x median, 16 wines, median 14 years apart (premier cru about 2.0x; Gouges Clos des Porrets 1997 to 2024: 2.4x) | none |
| B, the cellared bottle | How much has one bottle gained since it was released? | Hachette band at release against iDealwine estimate today for the same vintage, after inflation | Premier and grand cru 2.2x (60 wines) | Other French Pinot Noir 1.2x (11 wines) |
| C, today's shelf | How much more does an older vintage cost than the current release, both today? | Wine-Searcher lowest offers, older vintage against 2020 and 2023 of the same wine | Premier and grand cru +24% average (+14% typical); Bourgogne and village +54% (+37% typical) | Pinot controls about +50% for 2015 (thin) |

Consistency check: B ≈ A × C. For premier and grand cru, 1.9 × 1.15 to 1.25 ≈ 2.2. The three measures agree, so most of an old premier or grand cru's gain since release comes from the release-price climb, and a smaller part from age.

### 17.21 Pouilly-Fuissé premier cru as a precedent: quick check (28 October 2026)

Facts (green):
- INAO national committee approved 22 premier cru climats on 3 September 2020: 194 ha, about 24% of the appellation, across Chaintré, Fuissé, Solutré-Pouilly and Vergisson (INAO press release; Decanter, 18 September 2020).
- Labels apply from the 2020 vintage, no retrospective labelling. Premier cru maximum yield 56 hl/ha against 60 for village (Burgundy Report).

Forecast at the time (amber, one trade writer): Wine Review Online, late 2020: "consumers should expect to pay at least 20 percent more for Premier Cru Pouilly-Fuissé from top growers or négociants", while top sites "already cost more, even without the official Premier Cru designation". It also noted falling bulk prices, the 25% US tariff and Covid closures at the time.

Price evidence found (Guide Hachette cellar-door bands, Château de Fuissé):

| Wine | Status from 2020 | Before | After |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Le Clos Monopole | 1er cru | 30–50 € (2016, 2017) | 50–75 € (2021, 2023) |
| Les Brûlés | 1er cru | 30–50 € (2013, 2017) | 50–75 € (2022) |
| Les Combettes | village climat | 15–20 € (1998 only) | 50–75 € (2023) |
| Tête de cuvée | village blend | 20–30 € (2016, 2018) | 20–30 € (2020) |
| Saint-Véran | other AOC | 15–20 € (2017, 2020) | 20–30 € (2022) |

Reading: both promoted wines moved up one band. The non-promoted Combettes now sits in the same band, with no recent pre-2020 reading, so the jump cannot be pinned on the promotion. Other confounders: 2021 frost, post-2021 inflation, a band jump that can mean anything from about 0% to 150%.

Not available free: prefecture fermage prices for Saône-et-Loire give one Pouilly-Fuissé line (874.78 €/hl, 2022 harvest), with no premier cru split. Wine-Searcher region page refused automated access.

Verdict: not usable as evidence of a name premium. Usable as a fact-only precedent (Burgundy's most recent premier cru promotion, 2020). A proper test would need a paid same-wine price scan across several producers, 2016–2019 against 2020–2023.
